# Naming & Domain Strategy

Find a name the user can build equity in—not a word nobody has ever used. Treat naming as a narrowing funnel: define the job, generate widely, filter hard, then investigate only the strongest candidates.

This skill provides preliminary commercial screening, not legal clearance or legal advice. Use current web and trademark sources for live checks, cite material findings, and recommend a qualified trademark attorney before filing or making a high-cost commitment.

## Start with the naming role

Classify the target before proposing names:

- **Product brand:** optimize for memorability, easy speech and spelling, category fit, search ownership, trademark strength, and an exact customer-facing domain.
- **Umbrella company:** optimize for durability, commercial cleanliness, credible legal use, broad future scope, and a clean corporate domain. Brevity and an exact root domain matter less if customers mainly see product brands.
- **Internal experiment:** optimize for speed, clarity, and an inexpensive available domain. Avoid premature legal or premium-domain spending.

If the user has several products or may create them later, explicitly consider separating the legal entity from product brands. Do not force one name to solve both jobs.

## Build a compact brief

Capture or reasonably infer:

- role and audience;
- desired emotional tone and personality;
- relevant metaphors or conceptual territory;
- hard constraints: word count, syllables, letters, language, suffixes, domain extensions;
- crowded roots, clichés, sounds, or associations to avoid;
- examples the user likes and, crucially, *how those names work*;
- geographic markets, product category, and expected trademark ambition;
- current stage and realistic domain budget.

State consequential assumptions. Do not stall when low-risk details can be inferred.

## Generate broadly; show narrowly

Generate roughly 50–200 candidates internally when the request warrants a full search. Explore genuinely different lanes instead of mutating one favored root:

- coined or fanciful words;
- memorable compounds;
- metaphorical real words;
- real-word-adjacent constructions;
- evocative, celestial, technical, natural, or fantastical forms when the brief supports them.

Switch phonetic territory when a lane becomes crowded. Cull immediately for awkward pronunciation, unclear spelling, generic startup cadence, weak recall, excessive descriptiveness, unintended meanings, or obvious conflicts.

Show a curated shortlist, normally 5–15 names. Explain each briefly and honestly. Do not bury the user in the raw generation set, and do not let an available domain rescue a mediocre brand.

## Test candidates in use

Read every serious candidate aloud and place it in realistic sentences, adapting these to the business:

- “Have you tried ___?”
- “According to ___…”
- “___ raised a Series A.”
- “I use ___ for market research.”
- “___ is a product of ParentCo.”
- “© 2026 ___ LLC.”

Check likely pronunciation, stress, spelling after hearing it once, plural or possessive awkwardness, abbreviation, and whether the legal and product forms sound natural. Reject clever names that fail ordinary speech.

## Score before domain shopping

Use a 10-point scale and explain the decisive strengths and weaknesses. Weight the score for the naming role rather than applying false precision.

| Dimension | Default importance |
|---|---|
| Memorability | High |
| Pronunciation | High |
| Spellability | High |
| Distinctiveness | High |
| Search ownability | High |
| Preliminary trademark outlook | High |
| Long-term flexibility | High |
| Category fit | Medium |
| Domain situation | Medium-high |
| Visual identity potential | Medium |

For an umbrella company, raise durability and flexibility and slightly lower domain brevity. For a public product, raise memorability, search ownership, and exact-domain quality. Advance only genuinely strong candidates—roughly 8/10 or better—unless the user asks to see exploratory options.

## Run a light commercial screen

For finalists, use current sources to search:

- exact name and quoted exact name;
- name + product category;
- name + `software`, `AI`, `SaaS`, `technology`, `consulting`, or other relevant services;
- name + `trademark`;
- company registries, app stores, social platforms, and relevant marketplaces when useful;
- official trademark databases for the intended countries when feasible;
- visual, spelling, and phonetic neighbors.

Evaluate conflicts by similarity of name, goods or services, customers, channels, geography, and apparent activity. Similar name + similar market is a serious warning. Similar name in an unrelated field is context, not an automatic rejection. Distinguish exact-name results from neighbor results and flag uncertainty.

Never describe a light screen as definitive clearance. If the user is ready to form an entity, launch publicly, file a mark, or buy an expensive domain, recommend a jurisdiction-specific entity check and attorney-level trademark search.

## Check phonetic neighbors explicitly

Spelling tricks do not create meaningful separation when names sound alike. For each finalist:

1. List plausible misspellings, singular/plural variants, dropped or doubled letters, alternate endings, and homophones.
2. Search the strongest neighbors with relevant category terms.
3. Ask whether a person could hear one name and type the other, assume affiliation, or meet both in the same buying context.

A one-letter difference is a yellow or red flag when pronunciation and market overlap remain close.

## Evaluate search ownability

Keep legal risk separate from commercial friction. Ask whether the brand can plausibly dominate search engines, LinkedIn, app stores, AI answers, and social discovery for its commercial meaning.

Note existing noise, autocorrection, famous unrelated meanings, and likely confusion. Prefer names for which the relevant business meaning can become the user's, even when absolute global uniqueness is impossible.

## Apply domain standards by role

Verify availability and pricing live; do not rely on stale search snippets.

- **Product:** strongly prefer exact `brand.com` or an extension users will trust and remember. Exact `.ai` can be strong for a genuinely AI-native product. Treat awkward prefixes, hyphens, and misspellings as real acquisition and trust costs.
- **Umbrella company:** prefer `company.com`, but a clean `companylabs.com`, `company.co`, or comparable corporate form can be acceptable when products have their own domains.
- **Internal experiment:** use a standard-price domain or sensible variation until validation justifies more.

Consider spoken ambiguity, email usability, typo leakage, renewal price, seller legitimacy, transfer risk, and relevant handles. Do not imply a domain is secured until registration or transfer is complete.

## Match premium-domain spend to conviction

Use stage-sensitive guidance, not rigid limits:

- idea: registration price or inexpensive alternative;
- promising MVP: hundreds may be reasonable;
- validated product with strong conviction: a few thousand or more may be rational if the exact domain materially improves trust and acquisition;
- mostly invisible umbrella company: avoid large premiums unless the corporate brand itself will be public-facing.

Compare the premium with rebranding cost, customer confusion, paid acquisition, financing, and runway. Present spending as a business judgment, not a naming-quality score.

## Test metaphor depth and visual potential

Strong names often support a coherent system rather than a single clever association. For finalists, list the ideas the metaphor can sustain across positioning, product language, features, storytelling, and visual identity. Favor fertile metaphors, but reject ones that require a paragraph to understand.

Make quick wordmark, icon, or mood explorations when visuals would change the decision. Judge whether the *name* naturally produces a distinctive visual system; do not confuse one weak logo sketch with a weak name. Check small-size legibility and obvious symbol clichés.

## Separate entity and product architecture

When useful, show the structure plainly:

```text
Umbrella LLC
├── Product A — producta.com
├── Product B — productb.ai
└── Consulting or future ventures
```

The umbrella can own IP and contracts while each public product has its own name, domain, and trademark strategy. Test attribution language such as “Product A is a product of Umbrella.” Do not add `Labs`, `Group`, or `Holdings` unless it improves the intended public identity or solves a real availability problem.

## Present decisions pragmatically

For a shortlist, provide:

- a ranked recommendation;
- concise rationale and sentence-test observations;
- scorecard or compact comparison;
- domain status with time checked;
- exact conflicts and phonetic neighbors;
- green/yellow/red interpretation;
- next action for each serious finalist.

Use direct verdicts such as **go**, **go with a yellow flag**, **hold for deeper review**, or **drop**. Separate facts from inference. Avoid overstating legal certainty or demanding perfection.

## Stopping criteria

Stop broad generation when one or more names have:

- strong brand quality, usually around 9/10;
- acceptable preliminary clearance, usually 8/10 or better;
- a good, attainable domain for the role;
- no obvious same-category conflict;
- plausible trademark and entity-registration paths;
- user preference strong enough to justify deeper diligence.

At that point, move from brainstorming to confirmation: deeper clearance, domain acquisition, entity availability, and filing strategy. More options can reduce decision quality after a candidate clears the practical threshold.

## Concise algorithm

```text
1. Define role: product, umbrella, or experiment.
2. Define tone, metaphor, constraints, market, and budget.
3. Generate 50–200 names across several naming modes.
4. Cull weak, confusing, generic, and obviously conflicted names.
5. Run sentence tests and score the survivors.
6. Search exact commercial uses and relevant categories.
7. Generate and search phonetic and visual neighbors.
8. Assess market overlap and search ownability separately.
9. Verify domain availability, price, and usability by role.
10. Evaluate metaphor depth and visualize the strongest finalists.
11. Choose a leader and stop broad searching at the practical threshold.
12. Deep-clear the winner, secure the domain, confirm the entity, and file marks when appropriate.
```

Core principle: **optimize for a name the user can build equity in, not a name nobody has ever thought of.**
